Constitutional Foundations

Core principles that structure American democracy

01

Popular Sovereignty

Government authority is derived from the consent of the governed. The people hold ultimate political power and delegate it to elected representatives.

Principle
02

Separation of Powers

Power is divided among three co-equal branches — legislative, executive, and judicial — to prevent concentration of power in any single institution.

Structure
03

Checks & Balances

Each branch has specific powers to limit the others. Congress can override vetoes; the President nominates judges; courts can strike down laws via judicial review.

Structure
04

Federalism

Power is shared between national and state governments. The 10th Amendment reserves non-delegated powers to the states or the people.

Key Concept
05

Judicial Review

Established in Marbury v. Madison (1803). The Supreme Court can declare laws unconstitutional, asserting the Constitution as the supreme law of the land.

Landmark
06

Limited Government

Government power is constrained by law. No person or institution is above the Constitution. The Bill of Rights specifically restricts government action.

Principle
07

Republicanism

Citizens elect representatives to govern on their behalf rather than exercising direct democracy. Promotes stability and deliberation in lawmaking.

Principle
08

Supremacy Clause

Article VI declares the Constitution and federal laws the "supreme law of the land," taking precedence over conflicting state laws (McCulloch v. Maryland).

Structure

The Three Branches

Structure and key powers of each branch of the federal government

🏛️

Legislative (Congress)

  • Bicameral: Senate & House
  • Pass federal laws
  • Control the federal budget
  • Declare war
  • Override presidential vetoes (2/3 vote)
  • Senate confirms appointments
  • Senate ratifies treaties (2/3)
  • Power of impeachment
  • Senate: 6-yr terms, 2/state
  • House: 2-yr terms, proportional
🦅

Executive (President)

  • 4-year terms, max 2 terms (22nd)
  • Commander-in-Chief
  • Veto legislation
  • Issue executive orders
  • Appoint federal judges
  • Negotiate treaties
  • Grant pardons
  • State of the Union
  • Cabinet & federal agencies
  • Electoral College election
⚖️

Judicial (Courts)

  • Supreme Court + lower courts
  • 9 justices (not fixed by Constitution)
  • Lifetime appointments
  • Judicial review of laws
  • Interpret the Constitution
  • Original & appellate jurisdiction
  • Writ of certiorari
  • Rule of 4 to grant cert
  • Majority, concurring, dissent opinions
  • Stare decisis (precedent)

Key Vocabulary

Essential terms for free-response and multiple choice

Bicameralism
A legislature divided into two chambers. The U.S. Congress is composed of the Senate and the House of Representatives.
Filibuster
A Senate tactic to delay or block a vote by extending debate indefinitely. Requires 60 votes (cloture) to end.
Cloture
A Senate procedure to end debate (filibuster) with a vote of 60 of 100 senators.
Gerrymandering
Manipulating district boundaries to favor a particular political party or group. Named after Governor Elbridge Gerry.
Pork Barrel Legislation
Government spending directed at specific districts to benefit constituents and gain political support.
Iron Triangle
The stable policy-making relationship between congressional committees, executive agencies, and interest groups.
Mandate
A requirement imposed by the federal government on state or local governments, sometimes without funding (unfunded mandate).
Incumbent
The current holder of a political office. Incumbents have a significant electoral advantage due to name recognition and resources.
Writ of Certiorari
An order from a higher court to review a lower court's decision. The Supreme Court grants cert to about 1–2% of petitions.
Amicus Curiae
"Friend of the court." A brief filed by a non-party (like an interest group) offering relevant information or arguments.
Selective Incorporation
The process by which the 14th Amendment is used to apply Bill of Rights protections to the states on a case-by-case basis.
Political Socialization
The process by which individuals develop their political beliefs and values through family, school, peers, and media.
Linkage Institutions
Mechanisms that connect citizens to government: political parties, elections, interest groups, and media.
Realignment
A dramatic shift in the political coalition supporting a party, often triggered by a critical election (e.g., 1932 New Deal coalition).
